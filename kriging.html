GROUP DISCUSSION · EXPERIMENT 1

Understanding the Semivariogram

  1. Plot several observation pairs in each case. Does semivariance always increase as the distance between observations increases? Why or why not?
  2. Click 1 · Show all observation pairs in both sections. Why might observation pairs separated by similar distances have different semivariances?
  3. Click 2 · Group into 15 m lag bins in both sections. How does the average semivariance change with distance? Why is it useful to group observation pairs into distance bins?
  4. Before revealing the fitted models, estimate the range for each semivariogram based on the lag-bin averages. Which case (the first or second semivariogram) appears to show spatial dependence over a longer distance?
  5. Click 3 · Show fitted model in both sections, then turn on the Range labels. How do the fitted ranges compare with your estimates? What does the range tell you about spatial dependence in each case?

Reading a fitted semivariogram

This is the same fitted spherical model as Section 1 (Case A). At zero separation, the semivariance of a point with itself is 0; the curve begins at the nugget as separation approaches zero from above. Toggle the labels on either graph to locate the parameters.

Nugget: near-zero discontinuity. Sill: modeled plateau. Range: distance where this spherical model reaches the sill. These are model parameters, not direct readings from one pair.

How is ordinary kriging calculated?

For a constant but unknown local mean, ordinary kriging predicts ẑ(x₀) = Σᵢ λᵢz(xᵢ), with Σᵢ λᵢ = 1. The weights solve a covariance system built from the chosen variogram model and all active observations. They reflect both distance to x₀ and distances among observations.

The map uses an isotropic, stationary covariance model. The nugget is treated as independent observation noise. Kriging standard deviation is the square root of the model-based prediction variance for a new observation; it includes the independent nugget noise. It describes uncertainty under these assumptions; it is not a measured error or a guarantee of accuracy.
